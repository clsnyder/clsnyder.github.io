Dear [Editor]

Access & Routing

We need to make sure Jenn James /Rebecca Moody gives you editing privileges. There are several different categories of ‘ownership’ of the documents online: 

Authoring and Editing are the most common, followed by Production Management (preparatory for release for publication). When you are done editing, you can either route it to the Editor in Chief or to Jenn James. Comments can be inserted in the Discussion tab (below) prior to routing, or there is a comment box that appears when you actually route it. The former Discussion box is permanent, so comments like, “This is terrible” might be avoided…

Editing

You may see some green highlighted areas of text - these are editorial comments, and they don't show up in the published version, even if left in place (they are ignored). Nevertheless, I delete them once the author has responded (or if I just fix it myself). You can create these by typing out some text, click and drag to select it, and clicking the little thumbtack icon.

The red circle is the thumbtack icon for editing; the large red rectangle shows both a previously highlighted editorial comment (dark green) and another (“Test”) that is now light green, but will become a dark green editorial comment when the thumbtack is clicked.

There is a tab called History (see below) - once you go to that tab, there are two subtabs -  Stage and Detailed.

If you click the Detailed tab, and look at  "Editing Charles Snyder 2026-10-28 74 changes"  - click this button and you can see what I added or changed, color coded.

Style and Content

There is a separate Style Sheet for the NAT. Your job is mostly reviewing and adjudicating the content, but sometimes there are obvious errors or careless mistakes. These tend to become more frequent towards the end of long sections. There is a somewhat buggy spell check (see below). It is worth using, but turn it off when you add or alter text (the bugginess).

Learning objectives

The original schema for the NAT called for an attempt to ‘nuggetize’ the content by asking questions rather than stating facts: “What imaging should be obtained for a liver mass?” versus “A CT with Eovist should be obtained.” These learning objectives were to  be limited to five thousand characters (not including references) and five references.

References

Pay particular attention to the references - if the authors have failed to use the online reference tool and simply pasted them in: “The incidence is 1 in 5,000 [1-3]” or “Reoperation is needed about 5% of the time [Smith, Jones, Robinson et al…]”, just send it back to them to do it correctly.

The use of AI sometimes results in far too detailed information, with interquartile ranges and esoterica about trivial points. AI’s like to bold things, overuse bullet points, and love em dashes (–), BTW.

Undefined or overused acronyms are a common problem. Content experts often forget that the naive reader may not know what obscure acronyms mean. All should be initially defined

Glossary

This is underused. Often, relatively uncommon diseases are referenced in passing, “CLOVES syndrome and Proteus syndrome might be associated.” Assume the reader does not recall the details of these, and put it in the Glossary, with a link in the text to the Glossary so that the reader can refresh their memory without leaving the page.

Discussion Cases and Questions

I have been using Open Evidence to create multiple choice questions, answers, and discussions for the sections, with a disclaimer that states it was done with the aid of AI. They often need substantial revision, but these are useful.
